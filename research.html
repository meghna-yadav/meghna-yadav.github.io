Working papers & works in progress

  • Production of Bureaucratic Embeddedness: Evidence from Forest Rights Act in India

    Abstract

    Under what conditions do states administer territories through bureaucrats local to the region (insiders) versus outsiders? Insider bureaucrats have more local knowledge which can improve policy implementation but also face higher risks of capture by local populations. I argue that the amount of discretion bureaucrats have affects how politicians evaluate this trade-off. When discretion is low, insiders’ informational advantages dominate over capture risks. As discretion increases, the risk of capture increases, making the use of outsiders more appealing to politicians. I study this trade-off through India’s forest rights regime. The Forest Rights Act (2006) increased the discretion that district-level officials had in adjudicating claims to forest land by indigenous communities. I show that before the Act was implemented, districts with greater forest cover were more often staffed by insiders. After the Act these high-forest districts shifted toward outsider officials, but only in states whose economies were more dependent on forests. Policy reforms which expand frontline discretion can prompt principals to trade expertise for control, thus reshaping the state’s personnel infrastructure with downstream consequences for the provision of rights.

  • Roving Bureaucrats: Tenure and Use of Local Knowledge by Rural Development Officials in India

    Abstract

    Many bureaucracies manage the tension between delegation and control of bureaucrats through their rotation across posts and geographies. How do such rotation policies impact investments in different types of bureaucratic expertise? I argue that what officers come to know depends on how long they expect to stay: short tenures incentivise bureaucrats to prioritise portable, domain-specific expertise at the expense of local knowledge, which does not travel across postings. I study mid-level rural development officials in India who execute service delivery across multiple administrative units and policy domains. Through original survey data and a vignette experiment, I examine how they form expectations regarding their tenure and test how these expectations shape their investment in local knowledge. I further conduct a survey of elected representatives who rely on these officers for service delivery to measure the accountability of rotated officers to local actors.

  • Decentralisation and Investment in Legibility: Evidence from Local Governments in India

    Abstract

    Local governments often receive funding from the centre but rely on state-level bureaucrats for service delivery. Such funding can be conditional on transparency measures such as surveys of their jurisdictions. How does political alignment across levels of government shape the administrative data that local governments collect about their citizens? I argue that state governments have a partisan interest in such data collection, and the leverage to act on it through control of bureaucracy. I study this in the context of India’s Mission Antyodaya, where funding for local governments is contingent on conducting a survey of their jurisdiction and reporting results to the centre. Using a novel measure of information effort, I compare data reported over time across two sectors that differ in political value: social security policies, which deliver individually identifiable benefits, and women and child development indicators, which do not. States aligned with the party governing at the centre report more information on social security, but not on women and child development. Information collection also rises in aligned constituencies at more politically valuable moments, such as closer to state elections. Where local governments depend on higher-level bureaucracies, the partisan alignment of those higher levels shapes data transparency efforts, particularly for politically valuable information.
